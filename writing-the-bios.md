# Writing the bios

A place to work from. Nothing in this file affects the website — it's just
notes and examples for when you're filling in `js/rabbits.js`.

---

## Part 1 — Nine worked examples

These are the example rabbits the site was built with before your real ones
arrived. **They are invented** — no rabbit below exists. They're here purely
to show the shape of a bio that works: what to include, how long, and the
difference between describing an animal and selling one.

Read two or three, then write yours. Don't copy the words — copy the moves.


### Clover

> **A calm, biscuit-coloured lop who supervises everything from the sofa arm.**

Clover is the sensible one. She was hand-reared and has never met a person she didn't like, though she'd rather come to you than be picked up. She'll follow you from room to room, flop over dramatically when she's content, and she has strong opinions about coriander. She lives happily indoors and is fully litter-trained. Clover would suit a household that's around a fair bit and doesn't mind being gently bossed about.

## Part 2 — What makes those work

**The summary is one line, and it's the hook.** It appears on the card and
it's often all anyone reads. Give it one concrete detail, not an adjective
pile. *"A calm, biscuit-coloured lop who supervises everything from the sofa
arm"* beats *"a lovely friendly bunny looking for a home."*

**Name one specific, useless, true thing.** Strong opinions about coriander.
Binkies sideways for no reason. Rearranges the enclosure nightly. These are
what make a stranger picture a particular rabbit in their particular house.
Vagueness is what makes ten rabbits blur into one.

**Say the difficult thing plainly.** *"Not a beginner's rabbit."* *"She'd
rather come to you than be picked up."* *"No young children."* Every one of
those reads as trustworthy rather than off-putting, and each saves you an
application you'd have had to turn down anyway.

**Write the reader into it.** *"Someone to read next to."* *"An adopter who
finds her mischief funny rather than annoying."* People adopt the life
they're imagining, not the animal in the abstract.

**Length: three or four sentences.** Long enough to have said something,
short enough that people finish it.

**Don't oversell.** You want the right home, not the most homes. Every
overstatement costs you later — at the meeting, or worse, months in.

## Part 3 — Your ten

The site currently has a short description of how each one *looks*, written
from the photo, ending in a bracketed prompt. Replace the bracket with a few
sentences of your own. The questions below are just to jog your memory —
answer whichever have an interesting answer, ignore the rest.

For each rabbit:

- What do they do that none of the others do?
- Do they come to you, or wait for you to come to them?
- Are they easy to pick up, or emphatically not?
- What food makes them lose their composure?
- Where do they choose to sit when they could sit anywhere?
- Who in the family do they like best, and how can you tell?
- What would genuinely annoy them about the wrong home?
- Anything a new owner would be cross to discover later?

And a few that are specific to yours:

| Rabbit | Worth mentioning |
|---|---|
| **Bertie** | Six, and the oldest. What's he like now versus a few years ago? Older rabbits appeal to a particular sort of adopter — lean into it. |
| **Buffy** | Those ears. Also: is she one of the original rescues, or one of the babies? |
| **Cornel** | Five, and looks like the watchful one. Does he warm up, and how long does it take? |

## Part 4 — Two fields sitting empty

Every rabbit also has `likes` and `needs`, currently blank. They're quick
wins — `likes` shows as a little list on the rabbit's page, and `needs`
does more screening work than anything else on the site.

```js
likes: ["Dandelion leaves", "Cardboard boxes", "Being talked to"],
needs: "An adopter who'll get him neutered, and somewhere he can dig.",
```

Three or four words each for `likes`. One honest sentence for `needs`.

## Part 5 — Which two are the parents?

The site says a couple of them had babies. Right now nothing says *which*.
That's genuinely interesting to adopters — a mother and her grown kit, or
two siblings, are a natural pair to rehome together, and people like knowing
the connection. If any of them are related, it's worth a line in each bio.

If any two should go together, set it in both entries and the site flags it
everywhere automatically:

```js
bondedWith: "Misty",
```

Bear in mind the neutering point — an unneutered male and female can't share
a hutch, however fond of each other they are.
